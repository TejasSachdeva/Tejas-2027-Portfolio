Vision-Based Tactile Sensor

Mechanically Programmable Lattice Sensor for 3-Axis Force Estimation

Data Collection in Progress — Oct 2026

Project Overview

When a robot handles delicate objects, such as a wine glass or a ripe strawberry, it needs feedback on the magnitude and direction of the contact force. Without that information, it may damage the object or lose its grip. Tactile sensors measure these contact forces at the fingertip. Vision-based designs such as GelSight use a deformable gel above a camera, with a neural network estimating force from the observed deformation[1,2]. These sensors have been used in robotic manipulation and prosthetic applications, but the properties of a molded silicone layer are difficult to change after fabrication. Adjusting its stiffness requires making a new layer, and repeated loading can eventually damage the surface. One reported test found that the GelSight Mini lasted 1–3 hours under randomly directed loads of 10–30 N[7].

This project replaces the silicone gel with a 3D-printed polymer lattice. As the lattice deforms under load, an embedded camera records the change. A compact neural network then uses the difference image to estimate contact forces along three axes[3]. Changing the lattice geometry requires a CAD update and a new print, offering a more direct way to adjust the contact layer than reformulating and curing silicone. The sensor is intended for robotic hands and gantry end-effectors that require force feedback[4] — the same application space as GelSight, but with a mechanically programmable contact layer[5].

Why Lattices?

Most vision-based tactile sensors use molded silicone elastomers as their deformable contact layer[1,2]. Silicone performs well, but its stiffness is largely determined during molding. Repeated high loads can also damage the surface. Adjusting the sensing range requires preparing and curing a new gel, which can take considerable time for a change to a single material property. Increasing stiffness may improve durability, but it can also reduce sensitivity to small forces. With a homogeneous elastomer, these properties are difficult to tune independently[6,9]. The goal was to develop a contact layer whose mechanical response could be adjusted through its geometry instead[5].

A 3D-printed metamaterial lattice offers another approach. Its unit-cell geometry, strut thickness, and relative density can be adjusted to change effective stiffness, providing more design flexibility than a homogeneous elastomer[5]. Our original plan tested two lattice types: re-entrant auxetic and diamond. Auxetic lattices have a negative Poisson’s ratio (NPR), meaning they contract laterally under compression instead of expanding. This behavior was expected to concentrate deformation near the contact point and make displacement easier to identify in the camera images[11]. Diamond lattices have a positive Poisson’s ratio (PPR) and were the control geometry to isolate whether NPR actually produces a cleaner signal.

The auxetic design was ultimately excluded because of a manufacturing limitation. Kang et al.’s DLP printer can build re-entrant angles without support material, so their auxetic lattices came out intact[10]. Our SLA printer requires internal supports for the overhanging struts. Removing these supports from inside the lattice risks tearing the thin Flexible 50A struts, potentially damaging the specimen before testing. The study therefore shifted to diamond lattices, with strut diameter and top-plate presence varied to explore different stiffness configurations while remaining compatible with the printer.

Programmable Stiffness
Strut diameter shifts effective stiffness without changing geometry family. The 1.5 mm → 2.0 mm sweep covers roughly a 2× stiffness range and remains printable on SLA without support rework.
Camera Visibility
Diamond’s open, symmetric nodes deform visibly under both compression and shear without occluding adjacent struts. The full deformation field is visible to the embedded camera as a clean difference image.
SLA Printability
Diamond struts are self-supporting at the angles used here. No internal support removal is required, which was the critical failure mode that ruled out auxetic geometries on an SLA platform.
Rapid Iteration
Printing four specimens in Flexible 50A takes roughly five hours. Locating pins let specimens be swapped by hand, so a new geometry can be designed, printed, and tested the same day.

Hardware Design

The sensor housing consists of four main components: a top cover that holds the lattice, the lattice specimen, a mid-frame for the LED ring, and a camera base. Early prototypes were printed in white PLA, but internal reflections reduced image contrast. The housing was changed to black PLA to address this issue. Locating pins were added to improve alignment during assembly, and an external bracket replaced the lattice’s built-in brim, which did not remain secure under load.

Locating Pins
Aligns the top and bottom housing halves consistently. The pins also make it easier to swap specimens by hand and reduce setup variation between robot-arm runs.
Lead-in Chamfers
Chamfers around the screw holes help guide fasteners into place and reduce the risk of cracking the PLA, which occurred during early assembly.
External Brim Bracket
The lattice's built-in brim was too thin to attach rigidly to the housing. As a result, the boundary moved with the lattice and added noise to the camera signal. An external bracket now holds the brim in place so the measured deformation comes primarily from the lattice interior.
LED Illumination
An SK6812 LED strip with 144 LEDs/m surrounds the lattice window. Consistent lighting helps limit shadows and bright spots that could appear in the difference image and be mistaken for lattice deformation.

Lattice Specimens

Four diamond-lattice specimens were printed in Formlabs Flexible 50A resin using SLA. The material deforms under the planned loading conditions (0–20 N normal force and 0–5 N shear force) and is expected to recover between cycles, supporting repeatable data collection. The specimens form a 2 × 2 matrix crossing two strut diameters (1.5 mm and 2.0 mm) with two unit cell sizes (5×5×5 and 8×8×8 mm). All four specimens include a rigid top plate, which distributes probe contact across multiple unit cells and is expected to reduce stress concentrations at the loading point.

A — 1.5 mm strut, 5×5×5 cell
  • 40 × 40 × 15 mm overall
  • Softest configuration; smallest unit cell, thinnest struts
  • Highest cell count per footprint — dense deformation field
B — 1.5 mm strut, 8×8×8 cell
  • 40 × 40 × 16 mm overall
  • Soft stiffness with larger open cell geometry
  • Fewer cells; broader, lower-gradient deformation pattern
C — 2.0 mm strut, 5×5×5 cell
  • 40 × 40 × 15 mm overall
  • ~1.8× strut cross-section vs. Specimen A; increased stiffness
  • Dense cell layout with stiffer members
D — 2.0 mm strut, 8×8×8 cell
  • 40 × 40 × 16 mm overall
  • Stiffest configuration; largest cell, thickest struts
  • Most compliant under bulk compression; fewest cell boundaries

Vision Pipeline

The initial approach used marker tracking, but the pipeline was changed to image differencing. Each camera frame is compared with a reference image captured at zero load. The resulting difference image highlights changes in the lattice without requiring markers on its surface. A compact vision model uses this image to estimate Fx, Fy, and Fz.

Data Collection

Data collection uses a UFACTORY xArm 6, a six-axis force/torque sensor, and a 3D-printed probe with a rubber tip. The tip is designed to improve contact with the lattice and transfer shear loads while limiting slip. The planned protocol includes 2,400 loading cycles across normal and shear conditions. Each cycle pairs a force measurement with the corresponding camera frame.

Normal Loading
1,200 cycles from 0–20 N to evaluate force-prediction accuracy and consistency across repeated loading. Changes in the response over time may indicate fatigue.
Shear Loading
1,200 cycles with 0–5 N of lateral force applied under a normal preload. Rubber-on-rubber contact is intended to transfer shear while limiting slip.
Ground Truth
The xArm's 6-axis force/torque sensor provides force labels for each camera frame. An RSA-G2 Dynamic Mechanical Analyzer will be used for additional material characterization.

Preliminary Results

Before beginning the full 2,400-cycle protocol, the camera pipeline was evaluated under manual loading. In the clip below, the difference image updates as the lattice compresses and returns to its original shape. Changes are visible across the strut network, and the image returns close to its unloaded baseline when the force is removed. This preliminary check supports using image differencing for the planned robot-arm experiments.
